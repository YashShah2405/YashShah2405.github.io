Approach

How I work

The same four moves show up in every project, whether the system is a food plant, an engine line or a robot fleet.

01

Go and see

Start on the floor and in the data the operation already has. Ask where work waits and what it waits for.

02

Find the constraint

One step sets the pace for the whole system. Protecting it beats speeding up everything else.

03

Change the system

Fix the sequence, the layout or the information flow before asking people to work harder.

04

Make it stick

Standard work, a tracker people trust, and a number someone checks every day.

The line that was always late

Line 1 shipped 71% of its orders late. I found the bottleneck, fixed the order sequence in Oracle ERP and made material shortages visible every day. Late output fell to 35%.

Role

Production Planning and Supply Chain Intern

Where

Nityam Global Services, Ahmedabad, India

When

June to August 2025

Methods

Theory of Constraints, daily shortage review, ERP re-sequencing

Tools

Oracle ERP, Excel VBA, Power BI, Microsoft Fabric

71% → 35%Line 1 late output
78%less time to update the production tracker
80+stakeholders working from one current tracker
01 · Problem

Orders were late, and nobody could see it coming

Line 1 shipped 71% of its orders late, and customers waited weeks instead of days. Planners and more than 80 stakeholders worked from a manual tracker that took hours to update, so shortages and the bottleneck only showed up after an order was already late.

It was worth solving because the fix needed no new equipment. The lateness came from the order sequence and from slow information, and both were within a planner's control.

02 · Research

Two questions for every late order

I started with data the plant already had: order history and status in Oracle ERP, plus the manual tracker. For each late order I asked where it waited and what it was waiting for.

  • Late orders piled up in front of one station. That station set the pace for the whole line, so it was the bottleneck.
  • Jobs reached the bottleneck short of material. The most valuable hours on the line went to waiting.
  • The tracker was a day behind. By the time a problem was visible, the order was already late.
03 · Strategy

Protect the bottleneck

Theory of Constraints says a line can only go as fast as its slowest step. An hour lost there is lost for every order behind it, while an hour saved anywhere else changes nothing. So every change had to pass one test.

Does this keep the bottleneck busy, on the right order, with all its material?

That test produced three workstreams: sequence (which order runs next at the bottleneck), supply (no job reaches it short), and signal (everyone sees the same current numbers).

04 · Exploration

The usual answers each had a cost

OptionWhat it costsWhy it falls short
Overtime on the whole lineLabor cost every weekExtra hours at non bottleneck stations add nothing to output
Expedite every late orderPlanner time, freightWhen everything is urgent, the sequence gets worse
New scheduling softwareMonths and budgetToo slow for a line that was late today

05 · Decisions

Four decisions and what each traded away

DecisionUser needTrade-off acceptedExpected outcome
Fix the sequence before asking for capacityOrders out on time without capital spendSome stations sit idle at times, which looks wasteful on a floor walkLate output falls fast with zero capex
Re-sequence inside Oracle ERP, not a side spreadsheetOne source of truth for planning and purchasingSlower to change than a spreadsheetPurchasing and the floor see the same priority

Automate the tracker with Excel VBA from Oracle ERP80+ people need the same current numbersVBA is brittle and has one owner, so I documented itUpdates in minutes instead of hours
06 · Solution

A line that runs in the right order

  1. Sequence. I re-sequenced open orders in Oracle ERP so the bottleneck always worked on the order that protected the most due dates.
  2. Supply. A daily shortage review checked material before release, so jobs no longer reached the bottleneck incomplete.
  3. Signal. An Excel VBA tool pulled order status straight from Oracle ERP, cutting tracker update time 78%. I then built an order tracker in Microsoft Fabric with task triggers that sent customers periodic WIP updates.
07 · Outcome

Late output fell by more than half

  • Measured: late output fell from 71% to 35%. Tracker update time fell 78% for more than 80 stakeholders.
  • Observed: customer delays went from weeks to days, and customers began receiving WIP updates instead of chasing status.

35% late is still too high. My next step would have been a weekly review of bottleneck load and a small time buffer in front of it.

What I learned: the fastest improvement on a struggling line is usually a planning change. Equipment and headcount come after the sequence is right.

Line Control Tower

The tool I wished I had at Nityam: a queue that tells a planner which orders are at risk today, why, and what to do. Before drawing a screen, I simulated the two features I could build first. The result reversed my plan.

Type

Self initiated concept for Throughline, a fictional AI production control startup

My role

Problem framing, simulation, product and UI design

Users

Production planners, plant managers, customer service

When

November 2026

Tools

Python and NumPy simulation, HTML prototype

11.5% → 1.7%late orders at 88% load, modeled
17.6%late with due date sorting alone: the feature I almost built first
3,000simulated runs across 4 rules and 3 load levels
01 · Problem

Planners find out an order is late after it is late

In plants of 50 to 500 people, planners still run the schedule from ERP exports in spreadsheets. Late orders and missing parts surface after the due date, when the only options left are overtime, air freight or an apology call.

Investors see a market here. Y Combinator has recently backed several companies building software for the manufacturing office, such as Korso, Arzana, Smartbase and Tiny. The open question is what a planner needs first.

ConceptThroughline is fictional. Every number in this case comes from a model with stated assumptions, not from a customer.
02 · Research

Starting from the loop I ran by hand

My evidence is the daily loop I ran at Nityam. It is one practitioner's experience, so I treated it as a set of hypotheses and tested the riskiest one before drawing any screens.

  • Pattern: lateness was decided days earlier, when a job was released without its material.
  • Motivation: planners want fewer surprises and fewer status calls. They do not want another screen to maintain.
  • Riskiest assumption: that smarter sequencing is the feature that matters most.
03 · Strategy

Three principles

  1. Exceptions, not everything. Show only orders that need a decision today. On a normal day that is a handful out of dozens.
  2. Every suggestion explains itself in one sentence. A planner will not act on a recommendation they cannot defend to a supervisor.
  3. ERP stays the source of truth. The tower suggests, the planner decides, and the change writes back to the ERP.
04 · Exploration

Three directions

Direction A

Drag and drop scheduler

Full control on a Gantt chart. Rejected: planners already lack time, and it rebuilds the ERP.

Direction B

KPI dashboard

Easy to build. Rejected: it reports lateness after it happens and asks nothing of anyone.

Direction C · chosen

Exception queue

A short ranked list of orders at risk, each with a reason and one suggested action.

05 · Decision

The simulation reversed my build order

I assumed due date sequencing was the core feature. I modeled a line with one bottleneck at three load levels, with 22% of orders arriving short a component, and tested four rules over 250 simulated runs each.

Sorting by due date on its own made lateness worse: 17.6% late against 11.5% for first in, first out at 88% load. Urgent jobs that were short of material blocked the machine. Flagging shortages a day ahead did most of the work, and the two together cut late orders to 1.7%.

DecisionUser needTrade-offExpected outcome
Ship shortage flags first, sequencing secondStop releasing jobs that will stallNeeds a purchasing data feed, the hardest integrationMost of the modeled gain in the first release
One sentence "why" on every suggestionDefend the change to a supervisorFewer, simpler rules the model can explainHigher acceptance of suggestions
One click accept or snooze, no auto applyPlanner stays in controlSlower than full automationTrust first, automation later
06 · Solution

The exception queue

Click any row to see the reasoning and the suggested action.

Line 2 · Press cell
Thu 20 Nov · Shift A · synced with ERP 4 min ago
1 late risk1 shortage1 promise change

  • Why: two orders due Monday sit ahead of it. Moving #48207 to the front keeps both Monday orders on time with 7 h to spare.

  • Why: releasing now would stall the press for about 14 h. Running #48219 instead keeps the press busy, and #48213 still ships Friday after the kit lands.

  • Draft update: "Your order #48195 will ship Tuesday 25 Nov instead of Monday. The delay is a late component from our supplier; everything else is complete."

41 orders on track · hidden

A planner's morning becomes three decisions instead of a spreadsheet rebuild. Status updates to customers are drafted the moment a promise date moves.

07 · Outcome

What the model says, and what a pilot would prove

  • Modeled: late orders fall from 11.5% to 1.7% at 88% bottleneck load, and from 23.3% to 6.9% at 94%.
  • Assumptions: one bottleneck, 3 h mean job time, 22% of orders short a part for 8 to 40 h, due dates 3 to 7 days out. The code is public so anyone can change them.
  • Pilot metrics: share of orders late, planner hours spent on tracker updates, hours from a shortage to a flag, and customer status calls per week.

What I learned: test the riskiest assumption before designing screens. The feature that felt most "AI" was the one that made things worse on its own.

Fleet Launch Kit

A deployment system for Kestrel Robotics, a fictional startup that rents robotic pack stations to ecommerce warehouses. It moves site surprises to week one and runs lab and site work in parallel. Modeled time from signed deal to live robots drops from 10 weeks to 7.

Type

Self initiated concept for a fictional robotics startup

My role

Deployment process design, critical path and Monte Carlo modeling, field documentation

Users

Application engineers, field deployment team, customer site manager

When

November 2026

Tools

Python, critical path method, Monte Carlo

10 → 7 wklikely time from signed deal to live, modeled
12.1 → 8.4 wk80th percentile across 20,000 runs

01 · Problem

Revenue starts at go live, and go live keeps slipping

Kestrel earns nothing until robots run in production. Each new site takes about 10 weeks from signed contract, and most of the slip comes from surprises found at install: dead Wi-Fi zones, missing power, carton sizes nobody mentioned.

For an early robotics company this is the whole business. Every week saved is a week of revenue, a happier first customer and a faster path to the next site.

ConceptKestrel is fictional and the 10 week baseline is a modeling assumption. The research below is real.
02 · Research

What deployment teams are asked to own

I read current deployment and application engineering job posts at robotics companies. Posts describe the work teams struggle to staff, so they are a good map of where deployments break.

SignalSourceDesign implication
Recreate the customer's environment in the lab before going on site, then hand off a documented solution and scheduleUltra, Application EngineerLab work can start at kickoff if samples arrive at kickoff
Confirm network credentials, docking locations and hardware readiness before deploymentSimbe, Deployment EngineerA readiness scorecard the customer fills in week one
Meet performance expectations before site acceptance testingOSARO, Deployment EngineerAgree on the acceptance test before anything ships
Commissioning gates with documented evidence and handoff ready artifactsMytra, Deployment EngineerFour gates, each with exit criteria
03 · Strategy

Find surprises in week one, not week six

  1. Move discovery left. A one page scorecard at kickoff surfaces Wi-Fi, power and carton issues while there is time to fix them.
  2. Run in parallel what does not depend on each other. Lab build and customer site fixes can happen at the same time.
  3. Agree on "done" before shipping. A pre agreed acceptance script shortens commissioning.
04 · Exploration

Three ways to go faster

OptionUpsideWhy not first
Hire more field engineersMore sites at onceCost grows with every site, and each site is still 10 weeks
Remote commissioningLess travelNeeds a mature product and stable sites
Standard playbook with gatesShorter time per site at almost no costChosen: the cheapest lever an early team controls
05 · Decision

Four gates with exit criteria

Gate 1 · Week 1

Kickoff

Scorecard sent, top 20 SKU carton samples requested, target units per hour agreed.

Gate 2 · Week 4

Lab proven

Target rate met on the customer's own cartons in the lab, on video.

Gate 3 · Week 4

Site ready

All 12 checks green, signed by the site manager.

Gate 4 · Week 7

Live

Acceptance script passed, then 5 days at target rate.

06 · Solution

The kit: scorecard, runbook and go live dashboard

The runbook walks the field team through each gate with the evidence it needs. The go live dashboard tracks four numbers the customer cares about: units per hour against target, uptime, interventions per 1,000 picks, and open punch list items.

07 · Outcome

Three weeks sooner, modeled

  • Modeled: likely time from signed deal to live falls from 10.0 to 7.0 weeks. Across 20,000 Monte Carlo runs with uncertain durations, the 80th percentile falls from 12.1 to 8.4 weeks.
  • Why it holds: the gain comes from structure. As long as lab build stays the longest task, site fixes and the survey fit inside it.
  • Pilot metrics: weeks from signature to Gate 4, share of scorecard items closed by week 3, first week uptime, and punch list items open at go live.

Tooling and safety at Cummins

A summer as an advanced manufacturing engineering intern on a midrange engine line. I redesigned crank press tooling with three drawings released to production, closed a hand safety hazard and reorganized two tool areas.

Role

Advanced Manufacturing Engineering Intern

Where

Columbus Midrange Engine Plant, Columbus, IN

When

June to August 2026

Methods

Tooling redesign, 5S, 5 Whys, Fishbone, standard work

Tools

AutoCAD Mechanical, SolidWorks

18%less scrap after the crank press tooling redesign
3drawings released to production
25%faster tool retrieval after 5S
01 · Problem

Scrap, a hazard and lost minutes

Three problems shared one line. Crank press tooling was a source of scrap. Operators using a pneumatic torque tool had an exposed hand hazard. And in the Innovation Center and the manufacturing engineering crib, finding the right tool took longer than it should.

NoteDesign details are Cummins confidential, so this case shows the process and results rather than the drawings.
02 · Research

On the floor first

  • Scrap: I traced scrapped parts back to the press tooling with the senior engineers who owned the line.
  • Hazard: I watched the torque tool in use to see exactly where a hand could be caught.
  • Tool areas: I mapped which tools were used most into a heat map of the space.
  • Oil leak: I ran a 5 Whys and Fishbone analysis on an engine oil leak to document the root cause.
03 · Strategy

Fix it at the source

Each fix went where the problem started: the tool that made the scrap, the guard on the tool that caused the hazard, and the layout that caused the searching. Inspection and reminders treat symptoms; design changes remove them.

04 · Exploration

Options I worked through

  • Tooling: design revisions modeled in AutoCAD Mechanical and reviewed with senior engineers before release.
  • Guard: a flexible TPU hand guard modeled in SolidWorks, so it protects without slowing the operator.
  • Layout: the heat map set where each tool should live, with the most used tools closest to the point of use.
05 · Decisions

Trade-offs I accepted

DecisionUser needTrade-offOutcome
Redesign the tooling instead of adding inspectionGood parts first timeEngineering review and release time18% less scrap; 3 drawings released
TPU guard instead of a rigid oneOperator safety without slowing the jobMaterial wears and needs replacingHazard closed
Layout by usage heat map, with SOPsFind the right tool fastTime to re-label and train25% faster tool retrieval
06 · Solution

Released to production

Three tooling drawings went into production use. The TPU hand guard went onto the pneumatic torque tool. Both tool areas were reorganized with 5S and written SOPs so the layout survives the next shift change.

07 · Outcome

Measured on the line

  • Scrap: 18% lower after the tooling redesign.
  • Retrieval: 25% faster after the 5S reorganization.
  • Safety: the hand hazard on the torque tool was closed.

What I learned: in a large plant, a design is only finished when it is released, trained and written down. The drawing is the middle of the job.
